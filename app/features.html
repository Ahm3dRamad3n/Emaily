Features

Everything that actually runs behind every send.

Nothing on this page is aspirational. Each capability below is implemented in the current backend, grouped by what it's for.

Provider routing

Send through the account you already own

OAuth2

Multi-provider OAuth2 routing

What it does: authorizes Gmail or Microsoft through a real OAuth2 consent flow — a popup opens, you approve, and Emaily receives tokens via window.opener.postMessage.

Who benefits: anyone who'd rather not manage SMTP credentials by hand.

Why it matters: tokens are refreshed automatically before every send when they're close to expiry, so a connection stays alive without you doing anything.

OAuth · gmail.send

Gmail integration

What it does: connects a Gmail account using only the gmail.send scope.

Who benefits: anyone already using Gmail for their project or business.

Why it matters: that scope can send mail and nothing else — Emaily is never granted read access to your inbox.

Microsoft Graph · Mail.Send

Microsoft Graph API integration

What it does: connects Outlook / Microsoft 365 via the Microsoft identity platform, using Mail.Send and offline_access scopes.

Who benefits: teams standardized on Microsoft 365 or Outlook.

Why it matters: same minimal-permission principle as Gmail — send-only access, nothing broader.

API key

SendGrid & Resend integration

What it does: connects a transactional email provider with an API key instead of OAuth — supported for both SendGrid and Resend.

Who benefits: teams already paying for a dedicated sending provider.

Why it matters: no OAuth consent screen to manage — connect once with a key and you're done.

App password

SMTP with an app password

What it does: connects any provider that issues app-specific passwords over SMTP with STARTTLS, for cases OAuth or an API key doesn't cover.

Who benefits: anyone on a provider outside the OAuth/API-key list above.

Why it matters: the password is encrypted at rest, not stored in plain text next to your project.

Enterprise-grade security

Security that doesn't depend on you getting it right

Symmetric HS256 JWTs

What it does: issues short-lived (10-minute) access tokens signed with HS256, each carrying a purpose: login claim that's checked on every request.

Who benefits: every authenticated user, transparently.

Why it matters: the purpose claim means a password-reset or email-verification token — even if leaked — can never be replayed as a login session. Refresh tokens rotate on use, and logout immediately blacklists the access token.

App Check with custom expected issuers

What it does: lets a template require an App Check JWT on public submissions, verified against a per-template symmetric secret, with an optional expected-issuer check you configure yourself.

Who benefits: forms embedded in apps that already issue their own attestation tokens.

Why it matters: forged, expired, or wrong-key tokens are rejected with a 403 before anything is processed.

reCAPTCHA v2 with hostname matching

What it does: verifies reCAPTCHA server-side against Google, then checks the token's hostname against your project's allowed domains — so a stolen token from another site can't be replayed.

Who benefits: any public-facing form, especially ones that have attracted spam before.

Why it matters: the hostname check is what stops a copy-pasted token from a scraped page.

Dual-key access modes

What it does: every project has a pub_ key and a sec_ key, and you choose FrontendOnly, BackendOnly, or Hybrid enforcement.

Who benefits: anyone deciding how exposed a project's submit endpoint should be.

Why it matters: a public form only ever needs the public key; a server-to-server integration can require the private one.

Escalating auto-ban abuse prevention

What it does: rate-limit violations accumulate a weighted score in a rolling window; crossing the threshold triggers an automatic ban that escalates — 1 minute, 5 minutes, 15 minutes, 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours, 7 days, 30 days — the more it keeps happening.

Who benefits: every project, without any configuration required.

Why it matters: a first-time mistake gets a minute-long timeout; a determined abuser gets locked out for a month, and admins are notified for anything over 24 hours.

Templates

A template editor that's actually a routing config

JSON & HTML template editor

What it does: templates store subject and HTML content with {{variable}} placeholders filled in from the submission payload, plus routing fields like reply-to, CC, and BCC.

Who benefits: anyone who wants one endpoint to power several differently-formatted emails.

Why it matters: the rendered HTML is sanitized after substitution, so a malicious field value can't inject markup into the final email.

Auto-reply chaining

What it does: a template can be configured to automatically trigger a second template as an auto-reply once the first send succeeds.

Who benefits: contact forms that want to confirm receipt to the sender automatically.

Why it matters: no extra API call from your side — it's configured once on the template.

Attachments

What it does: attaches static files to every email a template sends, up to a per-template count set by your plan.

Who benefits: templates that need to include a PDF, brochure, or similar file every time.

Why it matters: the limit is enforced per plan, so it scales with what you're paying for.

Privacy mode

What it does: when DoSaveInHistory is off, the submission payload and rendered HTML are never stored at all.

Who benefits: forms that collect anything sensitive and shouldn't leave a persistent copy.

Why it matters: the trade-off is real and visible — a private submission can't be inspected in detail or retried later.

Observability

Detailed email submission tracing

Full status lifecycle

What it does: every submission is tracked through Pending → Sent or Failed / Resent / Discarded / QuotaExceeded, queued asynchronously so your form's response isn't blocked on delivery.

Who benefits: anyone who's ever had a "did that actually send?" moment.

Why it matters: a failed send is visible immediately, not discovered from a complaint days later.

Raw body logs

What it does: submission details include the original payload and the rendered HTML body, plus any error message, so you can see exactly what was received and what was sent — unless privacy mode was on for that template.

Who benefits: debugging a "the email looked wrong" report.

Why it matters: you're looking at the actual data, not a guess reconstructed from your own template code.

One-click retry

What it does: a failed submission can be re-queued directly from its history entry, as long as it wasn't marked private or already sent.

Who benefits: anyone recovering from a temporary provider outage or a misconfigured service.

Why it matters: no need to re-trigger the original form submission to try again.

AI-generated summaries

What it does: after a successful send, an AI summary of the email's content is generated and attached to the submission record.

Who benefits: anyone triaging a high volume of submissions without reading every one in full.

Why it matters: a bullet-point summary at a glance, without opening the full email body.

Project-level controls

Every project is its own configurable unit

Domain allow-listing

What it does: for FrontendOnly and Hybrid projects, the request's Origin header is checked case-insensitively against a comma-separated allow-list you set per project.

Who benefits: any project embedded in a specific site or set of sites.

Why it matters: your public key only works from domains you've explicitly approved.

Key regeneration & access-mode switching

What it does: regenerate a project's public/private key pair at any time, and switch between FrontendOnly, BackendOnly, and Hybrid without recreating the project.

Who benefits: anyone rotating a key after it's been exposed, or changing how a project is integrated.

Why it matters: a compromised key doesn't mean starting over.

Plan-based limits, with graceful locking

What it does: every plan caps monthly emails, projects, services, templates, and attachments per template. If you're ever over a limit after a downgrade, the excess resources are locked rather than deleted, and can be unlocked again within your new plan's allowance.

Who benefits: anyone who downgrades and doesn't want to lose their configuration.

Why it matters: nothing is silently deleted for being over a limit.

Developer-focused workflows

Built to be called, not installed

One endpoint, no SDK

What it does: every submission goes through POST /api/submit/{publicApiKey} — a plain HTTP POST with a JSON body.

Who benefits: any language or framework that can send an HTTP request.

Why it matters: nothing to install, version, or keep updated on your side.

Asynchronous, queued delivery

What it does: submissions are processed as background jobs, so your form gets an immediate response while the actual send happens right after.

Who benefits: forms where a slow third-party mail provider shouldn't slow down the user's browser.

Why it matters: your endpoint's response time isn't at the mercy of Gmail's, Outlook's, or SendGrid's.
